## 1. Introduction

When I had to choose between the five ready scenarios for the Final Project, the
decision was quick: I chose **B2 — Internal IT Help Desk for Employees**.

This was not a random choice. It came directly from **more than 10 years of
hands-on technical experience** across several organizations, in different
environments, teams and systems. The IT Help Desk is not a training case for
me — it is the daily reality I have lived and supported for over a decade.

This file explains why I chose this scenario, what experience I brought to it,
and how that experience shaped the 5 templates I built.

---

## 2. My Background

I have worked in the IT field for **more than 10 years**, across multiple
organizations and environments. Over that time, I have been part of teams that:

- Supported employees who could not access systems, devices or applications.
- Handled tickets that ranged from one-line questions to long threads with
  several people and shifting priorities.
- Dealt with planned maintenance windows and the pressure of urgent outages.
- Walked non-technical employees through fixes step by step, often in plain
  language and sometimes in two languages.
- Respected data protection rules: never asking for passwords, never sharing
  personal data, always verifying before acting.

That experience gave me a clear mental model of **how an IT Help Desk actually
works** — its rhythm, its pressure points, its written tone, and the small
details that separate a good reply from a confusing one.

---

## 3. Why the IT Help Desk Scenario

I chose the IT Help Desk for four reasons:

### 3.1 It reflects my real work
The scenarios were realistic, but only one of them matched what I have done
professionally for years. Choosing IT meant I could build templates that look
and feel like the ones a real service desk would use — not a classroom
exercise.

### 3.2 The facts are natural to me
The seven facts in this scenario are not abstract to me:

- Support hours, response times and approval chains.
- The rule that passwords are reset only through the self-service portal.
- The rule that hardware orders are tracked by the procurement team, not by
  the help desk.
- The rule that urgent outages go by phone to the duty line, not by email.
- The rule that the help desk never asks for passwords or verification codes.

I have lived all of these. Writing the IT Facts block felt like writing the
rules of my own workplace, not inventing new ones.

### 3.3 The tasks cover the whole IT workflow
The ten B2 tasks cover the full arc of IT Help Desk work:

- Replying to a ticket (the day-to-day core).
- Summarizing a long thread (so the next technician does not start from zero).
- Routing tickets to the right team (Access, Hardware, Software, Network, Other).
- Writing a planned-maintenance notice.
- Turning a known fix into steps an employee can follow alone.

These are the exact tasks I have done or supervised countless times. The five
I chose to build are the ones I have seen cause the most friction when done
poorly — which is precisely why building them as tested templates matters.

### 3.4 It lets me show the safety layer
IT Help Desk work touches sensitive data all the time: device codes, user
accounts, phone numbers, emails. This scenario was the best place to
demonstrate the **three layers of defense** we learned in the training:

- **Prevent** — rules inside the template.
- **Detect** — a verification step before anything is sent.
- **Decide** — a person signs off every reply.

That combination is not a theory for me. It is what keeps a real service desk
trustworthy.

## 4. What I Brought From My Experience

Building the library was not just about following the guide. My 10+ years in
IT shaped three things:

### 4.1 The facts are grounded in reality
The IT Facts block in my library is invented for training, but every fact is
one I have worked with: 20-minute attention windows, 1-working-day response
targets, manager approvals for hardware, dedicated duty lines for urgent
outages. That grounding makes the templates feel accurate.

### 4.2 The rules reflect real risks
The five rules I wrote (Data, Verification, Fairness, Policy, Human decision)
are not abstract principles. They map to real risks I have seen:

- A leaked screenshot with a user's account name.
- A reply that promised a date the desk could not control.
- A ticket routed to the wrong team, delaying the fix.
- A decision made by an AI draft that should have been made by a manager.

Each rule answers one of those risks.

### 4.3 The templates speak like a real IT desk
From the greeting tone to the "next step" line, every template is written the
way an experienced IT desk actually writes to employees: polite, brief,
fact-based, and always closing with one clear action. That is a habit built
over a decade, and it shows in the library.

---

## 5. Why These Five Tasks

I built 5 of the 10 tasks. Each one was chosen for a specific reason:

| # | Task | Why I chose it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reply to a ticket | The most common daily task on any IT desk. |
| 2 | Summarize a thread | Saves the next technician time and prevents repeated questions. |
| 3 | Route a ticket | Prevents misrouting, which is the biggest source of delay. |
| 4 | Planned-maintenance notice | A high-risk task: a wrong date or time affects hundreds of employees. |
| 5 | Fix-it steps | Empowers employees to solve simple issues alone, freeing the desk for real problems. |

Together, they cover the whole lifecycle: **receive → understand → route →
announce → guide**.

## 6. What I Learned From the Project

Even with 10+ years in IT, this project changed how I think about AI in the
workplace. Three lessons stood out:

1. **Rules prevent more than they promise.** The templates do not guarantee a
   perfect reply. They lower the chance of a bad one. That is the honest
   standard for real use.

2. **Verification is not optional.** No matter how fluent the AI output is,
   someone still has to read it against the source. The CHECK section and the
   five-point checklist made that routine.

3. **A person still decides.** No matter how good the template is, the decision
   on eligibility, approval, or a complaint stays with a human. That is not a
   limitation — it is the design.

---

## 7. Conclusion

I chose the Internal IT Help Desk scenario because it is where I have spent
more than a decade of my career, because its facts and tasks mirror real work,
and because it let me show — not just claim — how responsible AI use looks on
a real service desk.

The library I built (5 approved templates, 1 Facts block, 1 Rules file, 1 test
report) is small, tested, and ready for a colleague to pick up on day one. If
a new agent can use it and get the same quality of reply without asking me,
the project has done its job.
